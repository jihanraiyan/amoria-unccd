# Amoria model assumptions

This document explains every model in `src/simulation/`: the equations, the units, where each relationship comes from, what the model can and cannot show, and which numbers are educational approximations. The authoritative list of values is `src/simulation/params.ts`. Every parameter there is tagged `evidence-informed` (the form or rough size follows a published method, named in its note) or `game-balance` (chosen so the game is playable and the tradeoffs are readable). `listParameters()` returns the full list with tags and notes for the in-game assumptions panel. When this document and `params.ts` disagree, `params.ts` is right and this document is out of date.

**The short version.** Amoria is a teaching model. It borrows the structure of real, widely used methods (the NRCS curve number for runoff, FAO-56 for water use, FAO-33 for crop response to water, RUSLE for erosion, D8 routing, InVEST-style sediment connectivity) and joins them so that cause and effect are visible. The numbers are in game units. The same model runs on the fictional procedural valley and on the real-data Kaani map in Machakos County, Kenya, which is the world players start on (section 20). On Kaani the terrain, land cover, river and roads come from real data, but the climate, soils and every rate are the same generic, game-balance values used on the valley. Neither map is calibrated to measured outcomes, and nothing here should be read as a prediction. Where we write "tonnes" for sediment or food, read "game tonnes": a consistent internal unit, not a measurement.

Sections 1 to 13 follow the order of one simulated season. Sections 14 to 19 describe mechanisms that cut across several models: the business-as-usual twin, spin-up and per-map calibration, household self-supply, grazing pressure and forest livelihoods, irrigation rules and the whole-game resilience goals. Section 20 covers the real-data Kaani map and section 21 how the goal targets were set against the strategy bots.

## 1. Space, time and state

- **Grid.** 64 x 64 cells, each 100 m x 100 m (1 ha). Index `i = z * width + x`. All layers are typed arrays (struct of arrays).
- **Timestep.** One step is one season: a wet season, then a dry season, so 2 steps per year. The guided scenario runs 20 years (40 steps); sandbox play continues past it. Inside hydrology each season is split into 3 sub-periods so that soil storage limits matter.
- **Water units.** Depths in mm over one cell ("cell-mm"). 1 mm over 1 ha = 10 m3. The ledger reports basin-mean mm (sum of cell-mm divided by the number of cells).
- **Authoritative state** (`WorldLayers`): cover, structure and density, cropland intensity, conservation, irrigation, cover age, soil health (0..1 index), root-zone soil water (mm), protective vegetation cover (0..1), shallow groundwater (mm), wetland ponding (mm), plus reservoir storage and reservoir silt. The state also carries the business-as-usual twin (`reference`, section 14), the per-map calibration (`calibration`, section 15) and the hectares of timber sold this season (`economy.timberSoldHa`, section 8). Everything else (fields, totals, diagnostics, community indicators) is derived each step and is never edited by an action.
- **Determinism.** All randomness comes from seeded mulberry32 streams (`rng.ts`), one stream per subsystem. The same world, seed and action log always give the identical state (tested bit for bit through serialisation). There is no `Math.random` or clock in the simulation.

## 2. Terrain (computed once)

- **Slope**: Horn (1981) 3 x 3 finite differences, rise over run.
- **Depression filling**: Priority-Flood with an epsilon gradient (Barnes, Lehman and Mulla 2014). Every interior cell gets a strictly lower neighbour on the filled surface, so no water is trapped in pits and every drop eventually leaves through a map edge, where the ledger counts it as outflow. The rendered elevation is not changed; only routing uses the filled surface.
- **Flow direction**: D8 steepest descent on the filled surface (O'Callaghan and Mark 1984). Edge cells with no lower neighbour drain off the map.
- **Routing order**: a topological order of the D8 graph (Kahn's algorithm), so each cell is processed after everything upstream of it. A cycle throws an error.
- **Flow accumulation**: number of upstream cells including the cell itself.
- **Floodplain height**: HAND, height above nearest drainage (Renno et al. 2008): follow the D8 path down to the first channel cell and take the elevation difference.
- **Bankfull capacity** (channel cells): 1.5 times the peak flow index of a reference normal wet season on the starting landscape (with a floor of 2 x the square root of flow accumulation). Rivers shape their channels to frequent floods, so a capacity tied to the usual wet-season peak behaves like a 1 to 2 year flood channel. This calibration is game-balance.

## 3. Weather and demography (exogenous)

Generated once in `createInitialState` from the seed. They never read player state; tests confirm that actions and steps leave them unchanged and that different strategies on the same seed see identical seasons.

- **Season kinds** follow a Markov chain: normal, favourable, drought, heavy rain. Droughts persist (a drought is followed by another with probability 0.5). Each kind multiplies the seasonal normal rainfall, potential evapotranspiration (PET) and storm intensity, with seeded noise (drought: rain x 0.45, PET x 1.12; heavy rain: rain x 1.75, storm intensity x 2.1). Normals: wet 420 mm rain and 430 mm PET, dry 110 mm rain and 750 mm PET. That is about 530 mm of rain a year against about 1180 mm of PET, an aridity index (P/PET) near 0.45, inside the UNEP semi-arid band of 0.2 to 0.5. The numbers describe a generic semi-arid valley, not a real station.
- **Guided scenario design.** On top of the random draws, the first 20 years always contain two calm opening years (no drought or heavy rain), a 3-season drought that starts in a wet season of years 6 to 11, and a heavy-rain wet season in years 3 to 8 that does not overlap the drought. The seed chooses which years. These seasons are flagged `designed: true`. This is scenario design so that every player meets the learning moments; it is not a claim about climate. Tested on 60 seeds.
- **Prefix stability.** Generating more years never changes earlier seasons, so the sandbox can be extended without changing the guided run.
- **Orographic rain**: rain on a cell = season rain x (1 + 0.0006 x (elevation - mean elevation)), clamped to 0.8 to 1.25. Game-balance.
- **Demography**: natural increase about 0.8% a season, plus regional migration pressure that rises to a peak around year 9 (a seeded wave). This is the would-be inflow; how many people settle depends on the valley (section 9).

## 4. Hydrology (`models/hydrology.ts`)

Each step, cells are processed in routing order. For every land cell and each of the 3 sub-periods (rain share wet 0.3/0.4/0.3, dry 0.5/0.3/0.2; PET split evenly):

1. **Roof tanks first.** On home cells, the rain caught by roof tanks (section 16) is taken out before the rest reaches the ground.
2. **Runoff from rain** uses the NRCS curve number method (USDA SCS 1986, TR-55; NRCS National Engineering Handbook Part 630). The season's rain falls as a fixed set of storms: events = (events per season x rain share) / storm intensity (24 events in a wet season, 8 in a dry one), with depth classes 0.4, 1.0 and 2.2 times the mean depth (shares 40%, 40%, 20%). Storm intensity makes fewer, bigger storms with the same total. For each storm of depth d:
   - S = 25.4 (1000 / CN - 10) mm, Ia = 0.2 S (TR-55)
   - Q = (d - Ia)^2 / (d - Ia + S) when d > Ia, else 0
   - **Curve numbers** come from TR-55 tables for hydrologic soil group B: woods 55 (good) to 66 (poor), pasture 61 to 79, straight-row crops 78 to 81, contoured and terraced crops 71 to 74, woods-grass combination (our agroforestry) 58 to 73, 1/8-acre residential 85 (homes at density 1), commercial 92 (denser homes, markets, waterworks), paved 98 (roads). Bare land runs from 86 (TR-55 bare fallow, group B) to 91 (the TR-55 bare-fallow value for group C, used here as a stand-in for a crusted, compacted surface). Wetland 70 to 80 is game-balance (TR-55 has no wetland class).
   - **Hydrologic condition** (0 = poor, 1 = good) = 0.55 x vegetation / full vegetation for that cover + 0.45 x soil health / 0.8 (game-balance weights). On bare land the condition is 1 - compaction instead (item 5). CN2 = poor + (good - poor) x condition.
   - **Slope adjustment**: CN2s = (CN3 - CN2)/3 x (1 - 2 e^(-13.86 slope)) + CN2, with CN3 = CN2 e^(0.00673 (100 - CN2)) (Williams 1995, as given in the SWAT Theoretical Documentation, Neitsch et al. 2011).
   - **Antecedent moisture**: CN1 = CN2 - 20 (100 - CN2) / (100 - CN2 + e^(2.533 - 0.0636 (100 - CN2))) (SWAT documentation). We interpolate CN1, CN2, CN3 by root-zone wetness and apply the adjustment at half strength, because CN moisture classes are defined for about 5 days of prior rain while our soil store is seasonal (game-balance).
3. **Run-on infiltration**: a share of the surface water arriving from upslope soaks in (forest 0.5, agroforestry 0.45, grassland 0.3, cropland 0.25 or 0.4 with conservation, bare 0.1, built 0.05, wetland 0.6), capped at 25 mm per sub-period times the dry fraction of the soil. Game-balance.
4. **Root zone**: capacity = 130 mm x rooting factor x (0.55 + 0.45 x soil health). Rooting factors: forest 1.4, agroforestry 1.25, wetland 1.2, cropland 1.0, grassland 0.9, bare 0.6, built 0.25. This follows the FAO-56 idea TAW = (field capacity - wilting point) x root depth; the numbers are game-balance. A young forest or agroforestry planting roots like grass at first and deepens to the tree value in proportion to its maturity (section 6), so planting does not suddenly change the water the root zone holds.
5. **Compaction of bare land** (`HYDRO.compaction`). Compaction = 1 - vegetation / 0.14 on bare land, and 0 on every other cover. Freshly cleared or overgrazed bare land (compaction near 1) has lost the root channels and crumb structure that let water soak in deeply. Three effects, all scaled by compaction: the curve number moves toward its poor value (91); up to 60% of the water the shallow root zone cannot hold in that sub-period runs off over the surface (saturation excess) instead of percolating; and preferential recharge falls to 35% of its normal share. When bare land regrows a little cover (or turns back into grassland by succession, section 6) the effects fade. This is the infiltration side of the trade-off described by Bruijnzeel (2004). The magnitudes are game-balance.
6. **Infiltration** = rain - runoff, plus run-on.
7. **Recharge** to shallow groundwater: 0.16 x (1 - 0.65 x compaction) x infiltration x wetness (preferential flow that bypasses the root zone), plus anything above capacity.
8. **Evapotranspiration** (FAO-56 idea, Allen et al. 1998): ETm = Kc x PET; ETa = min(soil water, ETm x Ks) with Ks = min(1, soil water / ((1 - p) x capacity)), p = 0.5. Season-average Kc: forest 0.9, agroforestry 0.9, grassland 0.75, cropped field 0.9 (x1.1 at high intensity), fallow field 0.3, wetland 1.05, open water 1.05, built 0.25, bare 0.3. Kc scales from the soil-evaporation floor (0.3) to the full value with vegetation cover.
9. **Baseflow**: shallow groundwater drains as a linear reservoir, 35% a season, into the cell's subsurface outflow. This carries wet-season recharge into the dry season. Groundwater does not move sideways between cells except through this outflow.
10. **Wetlands** capture 60% of passing surface water up to a 400 mm store, lose it to evaporation (up to the wetland Kc), and release 30% a season as slow flow. They lower flood peaks and keep some flow going in the dry season.
11. **Channels and lakes** pass water on and lose open-water evaporation, 1.05 x PET x wetted share. On a river cell the wetted share = min(1, (flow / (3 mm x upstream cells))^0.5) per sub-period. A low river is narrow, so it loses less to evaporation than a full one. The 0.5 exponent is a game-balance choice: about 0.5 is the width exponent Leopold and Maddock (1953) found in the downstream direction (between rivers of different size), while at a single station, which is what this rule describes (one cell as its flow changes over time), they found about 0.26. We use 0.5 so that low rivers shrink faster. Lakes keep their full surface. The 3 mm reference is game-balance too. Without this rule every 1 ha channel cell evaporated like a hectare of open water, which emptied the dry-season river so completely that small changes in baseflow decided whether it ran at all.
12. **Reservoirs** (one shared pool, 120 000 m3 per reservoir cell minus silt) let 15% of inflow pass as an environmental release, store the rest up to capacity, spill the excess, and lose open-water evaporation.
13. **Withdrawals.** Section 16 (households) and section 18 (irrigation).
14. **Flooding.** Peak flow index = max over sub-periods of (quick flow x storm intensity^0.5 + baseflow). Flood ratio at a river cell = peak / bankfull. Floodwater depth = min(4.5 m, 1.1 m x (ratio - 1)). A land cell within 5 cells of the river floods when that depth exceeds its HAND. This is a screening rule for which low cells get wet in big events, not a hydraulic flood model.

**Water ledger.** Every step: rain = ET + household withdrawals (piped, roof tanks and wells) + outflow + change in root-zone soil water + change in groundwater + change in wetland storage + change in reservoir storage + residual. Irrigation is an internal transfer: it is taken from the river or reservoir (so it lowers outflow or storage) and appears again as ET, soil water or groundwater, so it is reported (`irrigationMm`) but not subtracted twice. Water stranded by land-use change (soil water under a new reservoir, ponding on a drained wetland, storage above a reservoir's reduced capacity) is released into the flow that step, so it stays in the books. Tests check that the residual stays below 0.001 mm every step through 20 years of random play, in isolated runs with reservoirs, irrigation and overfull stores, with no waterworks at all (households on tanks and wells only), and with compacted bare land.

## 5. Soil and erosion (`models/soil.ts`)

**Erosion index** per cell and season, in game tonnes per ha, with the RUSLE structure (Wischmeier and Smith 1978, USDA Agriculture Handbook 537; Renard et al. 1997, Agriculture Handbook 703):

A = R x K x LS x C x P, then a soft ceiling: E = A up to 150; above 150, E = 150 + 1350 x (1 - e^(-(A - 150) / 1350))

The ceiling is smooth (slope 1 at the knee) and always rising, so a harder storm, a steeper slope, less cover or no conservation always gives more erosion; it only compresses extreme values on bare steep land, and E never reaches 1 500. (An earlier version used a hard cap at 150, which made 1 to 6% of the steep cells on the Kaani map stop responding to storms and cover change.) Both numbers are game-balance (`SOIL.maxErosion`, `SOIL.erosionCeiling`).

- **R** = 1.0 x rain (mm) x storm intensity x run-on factor. Borrowed: erosivity grows with rain amount and intensity. Modified: a simple game-unit product, not EI30. The run-on factor (section 14) raises R on cells that receive more surface water from upslope than they would have under business as usual, the MUSLE idea that erosive energy grows with runoff (Williams 1975).
- **K** = 0.032 x (1.3 - 0.6 x soil health). A loam K is near 0.03 in SI units; degraded soil with less organic matter is more erodible, as in the Wischmeier nomograph trend.
- **LS** = (As / 22.13)^0.4 x (sin b / 0.0896)^1.3, the unit-stream-power form (Moore and Burch 1986), with specific catchment area As = min(flow accumulation, 3) x 100 m. The cap stands in for the point where sheet flow becomes channel flow.
- **C** = (1 - sealed share) x (Cmin + (1 - Cmin) x e^(-4.5 x vegetation)). The exponential follows the RUSLE surface-cover subfactor; the coefficient and floors are game-balance (forest 0.001, wetland 0.001, grassland 0.01, agroforestry 0.05, cropland 0.16 / 0.22 / 0.32 for low / medium / high intensity, bare 0.45). **Built cells** erode only on their open share: roofs and paving cover 30%, 50% or 70% of a home cell at density 1, 2 or 3, half of a rural earth road, 70% of a market or waterworks and 30% of other built land. The open share (yards, verges, unpaved tracks) erodes like grassland with the cell's vegetation.
- **P**: contour farming and terraces on cropland follow the contouring table of Agriculture Handbook 537 by slope: 0.6 at 1 to 2%, 0.5 at 3 to 8%, 0.6 at 9 to 12%, 0.7 at 13 to 16%, 0.8 at 17 to 20% and 0.9 at 21 to 25%, interpolated between class midpoints. The table stops at 25%; above it we assume contour practice loses its effect, reaching P = 1 at 30% (a conservative game-balance choice: real bench terraces can do better on steep land, but they are not modelled separately). Agroforestry gets P = 0.7 (tree rows as partial barriers, game-balance). Otherwise P = 1.

What is omitted: rill and gully processes, snow, channel erosion, slope shape, real soil texture data.

**Sediment routing.** In routing order, each cell receives sediment from upstream, deposits a share and passes the rest plus its own erosion downstream. Trap share: (0.05 + 0.55 x vegetation) x e^(-slope / 0.1) on open land, 0.6 in wetlands, 0.05 on built land, 0 in channels. The idea that vegetated buffers and flat ground between a hillside and the river cut delivery comes from the connectivity approach in the InVEST Sediment Delivery Ratio model (Natural Capital Project, InVEST User Guide); our trap rule is a simplification, not the InVEST equations. **Reservoirs** keep 0.9 x r / (r + 0.05) of incoming sediment, where r is storage capacity over the season's inflow. Brune (1953) showed reservoir trap efficiency rising with the capacity-to-inflow ratio; this curve has that shape but its numbers are game-balance. (During spin-up, which has no inflow figure for reservoirs, a fixed 0.9 is used.) Mass balance (eroded = deposited + leaving the map) is tested. Sediment trapped in reservoirs becomes silt at 1.3 t/m3 and reduces their capacity.

**Soil health** (0.05 to 1, an index, not a lab value). Each season, on unsealed land:

SH' = SH - (0.00015 x E + land pressure) x taper(SH) + recovery, with taper(SH) = 1 - e^(-(SH - 0.05) / 0.1)

- **taper** (soft lower bound, `SOIL.lossTaper`, game-balance): about 1 on any soil above 0.4, 0.39 at 0.1 and 0 at the 0.05 floor, because a soil with almost no topsoil or organic matter left has little more to lose. A single season's loss is also kept below 90% of the distance to the floor. Cells therefore settle above the floor, where they still respond to storms, cover and conservation, instead of sticking to it.

- **land pressure** on cropped fields: low 0, medium 0.0012, high 0.006 a season, halved with conservation (nutrient mining and tillage); on grassland, grazing pressure of 0.0005 a season (trampling and bite) x (1 + 4 x overgrazing), where overgrazing is how far the herd has outgrown its fodder land (section 17)
- **recovery** = 0.08 x vegetation x (0.5 + 0.5 x water factor) x (SHmax - SH) when below the cover's maximum. Maxima: wetland 0.86, forest 0.84, agroforestry 0.80, cropland with conservation 0.76, grassland 0.68, cropland 0.66, built 0.5, bare 0.40. With typical cover this gives a soil time scale of roughly 8 years under forest and 20 years on farmland.
- above the maximum (for example right after clearing forest), soil loses 2% of the excess a season (organic matter loss after conversion)

All of these are game-balance. Starting soil health is solved per cell so that it is in balance with the starting land use and the long-run climate (section 15), so the valley does not improve or decline on its own; change comes from land use and storms. Soil health feeds back into runoff (curve number condition), water holding capacity, erodibility and crop yield.

## 6. Vegetation and restoration (`models/vegetation.ts`)

Protective cover moves toward a target each season: up by 35% (trees), 50% (wetland) or 70% (crops and grass) of the gap, down by 20%, 40% or 60% of the excess. Target = cover-specific value for the season (forest 0.92 wet / 0.88 dry, grassland 0.68 / 0.45, cropped field 0.62 / 0.58, fallow field 0.18 or 0.32 with conservation residue, agroforestry 0.80 / 0.68, wetland 0.90 / 0.82, bare 0.14 / 0.08, homes 0.12, roads 0.04) x water limit (0.35 + 0.65 x water factor, except irrigated crops) x soil limit (0.5 at soil health 0 rising to 1 at 0.6). On open pasture the target is also divided by 1 + 0.6 x overgrazing (section 17), so overgrazed grass holds less cover.

**Restoration takes time.** For planted forest, agroforestry and restored wetland, canopy share = establishment + (1 - establishment) x maturity, with maturity = cover age / maturity seasons (forest 20 seasons = 10 years, agroforestry 12 = 6 years, wetland 6 = 3 years) and establishment shares 0.25, 0.30 and 0.30.

**Understory.** A young planting keeps a grass and herb layer on the ground its canopy does not yet cover:

target = mature target x canopy share + (1 - canopy share) x grassland target for the season

and the herb share of the cover grows and dies back at herb rates (growth and dieback rates are blended by canopy share). Planting seedlings into a grass sward therefore does not strip it; the herb layer fades as the canopy closes. The tests plant 20 steep grassland cells and compare them with the same cells left as grass through 10 years of the same weather: planted forest never erodes more than the grass in any season; young agroforestry erodes up to about 6% more in its first dry seasons (its young tree rows shed slightly more runoff onto the cells below than the grass sward did), but less in every year as a whole, and both cut total erosion on those cells by more than 20%.

Young plantings in severe water stress (water factor below 0.35, maturity below half) lose an extra 0.08 cover. Bare land left for 4 years with some regrowth (vegetation 0.12) and soil health of at least 0.3 turns back into grassland (natural succession). All game-balance, chosen so restoration is visibly slow.

## 7. Agriculture (`models/agriculture.ts`)

Per cropped cell and season (SPEC 7.5):

Harvest = area (ha) x potential yield x soil factor x water factor x management factor x event factor

- Potential yield: 2.4 t/ha in the wet season, 2.0 t/ha in the dry season. Rainfed fields are fallow in the dry season; only irrigated fields are cropped then.
- Soil factor = clamp(0.25 + soil health, 0.25, 1.05).
- Water factor = 1 - Ky (1 - ETa / ETm), from FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 33 (Doorenbos and Kassam 1979), Ky = 1.25 (their value for maize). ETa / ETm comes straight from the hydrology step. The same relative-ET idea underlies AquaCrop (FAO Paper 66); we use only the simpler seasonal form.
- Management factor = intensity factor (low 0.7, medium 1.0, high 1.35), scaled back toward low when the budget could not fund the farm program, x 0.95 with terraces (land lost to bunds).
- Event factor = 0.35 on a flooded field, times a burial loss of (deposit - 25) / 300 (at most 40%) when more than 25 game tonnes of sediment was deposited.
- **Agroforestry**: staple at 0.5 x potential with Ky 0.9 (shade and deeper tree roots buffer the crop), plus tree products worth 300 a ha a season once trees mature (ramping from 3 to 7 years after planting) x (0.6 + 0.4 x water factor) x soil factor x event factor. Game-balance.
- **Grazing and forest products**: section 17.
- **Crop price**: 260 a tonne, raised 8% per market (up to 3 markets).

**Drought acts only through these factors.** A drought lowers rain and raises PET; hydrology then gives a lower ETa / ETm, which lowers the water factor. There is no separate drought penalty and no penalty tied to any action. Tests check that the drop in harvest in a drought step is fully carried by the water factor, and that two different land states face the identical weather.

**Expansion vs intensification.** Expansion adds cropped area (and loses forest or grassland, with all the hydrology and soil effects of that). Intensification raises the management factor, the input bill (0, 15 or 55 a ha a season), the crop's water use (Kc x 1.1, so more irrigation demand and more water stress when rainfed), the erosion C factor and the soil pressure. Both are tested to raise food, by different routes and with different costs.

## 8. Economy (`models/economy.ts`)

The budget is a community development fund. Starting budget 300 000. Everything here is game-balance.

- **Capital costs** per cell: road 2 500 x (1 + 3 x slope), bridge 9 000 (a road on a river channel or open water; the cell stays water, so the water, soil and plant models skip it exactly as before, and it keeps the road upkeep), home 9 000 (upgrade 12 000; +2 000 on forest), cropland 2 500 (+2 000 to clear forest, +2 000 to drain wetland, +1 800 for irrigation), agroforestry 4 500, reforest 3 000, wetland 6 000, conservation 1 500 x (1 + 2 x slope), reservoir 60 000, waterworks 45 000, market 30 000, demolish 1 000, clear 1 100, change intensity 300, turn on irrigation 1 800.
- **Timber.** Clearing forest or agroforestry (with the clear action, or by building homes or fields on it) sells timber worth 1 900 or 800 x vegetation per ha at the full price. The local timber market is small: the price per hectare falls as more is sold in the same season, price = full price / (1 + ha already sold this season / 10). The revenue from a sale of h ha after s ha this season is therefore full value per ha x 10 x ln((1 + (s + h)/10) / (1 + s/10)). The 10th hectare of a season fetches half price and the 30th a quarter, and the count resets each season. Revenue grows only with the logarithm of the area sold, so liquidating the forest is not a money machine: past the first few hectares a season, clearing costs more than the timber earns.
- **Income each season**: a cooperative levy on crop and tree product value (the starting rate is set per map at game start, between 5% and 30%, section 15; about 11.5% on the procedural valley and about 6.3% on Kaani), local tax of 7 per resident at the starting rate, a water tariff of 0.2 per m3 of piped water, and market fees (1.5 per resident x the square root of up to 3 markets, plus 3% of crop value). Grazing and forest product value go to households, not through the levy.
- **Costs each season**: maintenance (road 12 a cell, homes 25 per density level, reservoir 900, waterworks 1 500 plus 0.06 per m3 treated times a turbidity multiplier, market 700, restored wetland 30, terraces 15, irrigated field 20 plus 0.02 per m3 delivered), services of 4 per resident, the farm input program, protection of young plantings for 4 seasons (25 a cell), flood repairs (2 500 per flooded home density level, 600 per flooded road) and loan interest (1.5% a season).
- **The levy lever** (`set-levy` action, `ECON.ratePolicy`, game-balance). The player can set the cooperative levy anywhere from 2% to 35% at no cost, at any time; it stays in force until changed (`economy.levyRate`, missing means the starting rate). The local tax per resident moves by the same multiple of its starting value: at twice the starting levy, residents pay 14 a season. The money is real and so is its cost. Farm households keep 1 - levy of their sales, and every resident pays the tax above the starting rate out of household income, so livelihood falls (section 9). Newcomers are put off: arrivals are multiplied by 1 - 0.35 x (rate / starting rate - 1), kept between 0.4 and 1.25. This is the SimCity-style loop the game was missing: a richer fund lets the town build, restore and repay, while a lower rate keeps households better off and draws more people. Bot results: the balanced plan at 2.5 times the starting levy (funded balanced, sections 11 and 20) keeps an operating surplus of about 45 000 a year on the valley and 107 000 on Kaani over years 6 to 20, against about -4 000 for the same plan at the starting rate, and pays with mean livelihood 0.82 instead of 0.89 (valley) and 0.78 instead of 0.85 (Kaani), which fails the wellbeing goal's no-part-below-0.8 rule. The coefficients (the 1 to 1 link of tax to levy, 0.35 and the bounds) are game-balance, not estimates.
- **Turbidity**: treatment cost multiplier = 1 + min(2.5, 0.3 x sediment tonnes per 1 000 m3 passing the intake). Upstream erosion therefore raises the water bill.
- **Drinking-water quality** (`models/waterQuality.ts`): the same intake sediment concentration decides how much piped water is safe to drink. Up to 15 t per 1 000 m3 the waterworks clean it fully; above that the unsafe share rises linearly to 40% at 80 t per 1 000 m3. Household water security counts only safe water: roof tanks and wells + piped water x (1 - unsafe share). Upstream erosion therefore lowers water security as well as raising the bill; trees, terraces and wetlands upstream protect it. Game-balance thresholds (`WATER_QUALITY`).
- **No negative budget.** If bills exceed budget plus income, everything is paid pro rata, the budget is set to 0, the unpaid amount is recorded, unpaid interest is added to the debt, and next season waterworks capacity and farm programs run at the paid share (`serviceLevel`). The farm input bill follows the same share: each cropped cell is billed its input cost times the service level, and its management factor sits the same fraction of the way from the low-input value to the full one, so players only pay for seed and fertiliser that was delivered. The only way to spend money you do not have is an explicit loan (limit 400 000). Tests check the budget is never negative through random play and forced overspending.

## 9. Community (`models/community.ts`)

All indicators are 0..1 and derived from the systems above; no action sets them directly.

- **Housing capacity** = residents per home cell by density level 1, 2 or 3: 25, 55 or 90 people times the map's household scale (section 15), rounded. On the procedural valley the scale is 1 (25, 55 and 90); on Kaani it is about 1.14 (28, 63 and 102). `state.calibration.residentsPerHome` holds the values in use.
- **Population**: births = population x natural rate x min(1, food security / 0.8). Arrivals = population x migration pressure x 1.3 x attractiveness^2 x the levy factor (1 at the starting rate, section 8), where attractiveness = 0.35 jobs + 0.25 food + 0.25 water + 0.15 safety. Departures = population x 0.04 x max(0, (0.55 - wellbeing) / 0.55), plus up to 3% more when food security is below 0.6. Population cannot exceed 105% of capacity; people turned away join a waitlist that fades by 15% a season. Housing adequacy = capacity / (population + waitlist).
- **Food security** over a two-season window (one main harvest feeds the year): (local food this season and last + bought food) / (2 x population x 0.19 t). Households can buy food from outside for at most 30% of the need, scaled by livelihood (poorer households can buy less). Bought food is a real cost: households pay 420 a tonne (the local farm-gate price is 260) out of their income, half of the window's purchase each season (`foodImportCost`), before livelihood is scored. So a town that outgrows its farms is still partly fed, but its livelihoods and wellbeing fall.
- **Food self-sufficiency** = local food this season and last / the same need, without bought food (0 to 2, `foodSelfSufficiency`). It is the measure behind the food goal (section 10). Doubling the starting town on the starting farms drops it to about 0.65 in a normal season, while food security, propped up by bought food, stays near 0.94 (tested).
- **Water security** = household water delivered (piped plus roof tanks and wells, section 16) / household demand.
- **Livelihood** = 0.6 x employment rate (jobs / labour force, labour force 42% of residents) + 0.4 x income index (household income per resident / 110). Household income = farm and tree product value after the levy, plus grazing and forest product value (section 17), averaged over two seasons, plus 220 per filled non-farm job, minus what households spent on bought food, minus the local tax they pay above the starting rate (negative, so a gain, when the player cuts the levy below it). Jobs per cell: cropland 1.2 / 1.5 / 1.8 by intensity (+0.5 irrigated), agroforestry 2.0, grassland 0.25, wetland 0.3, forest 0.08, market 30, waterworks 6, reservoir 2, plus 0.12 local service jobs per resident.
- **Hazard exposure** = 0.45 x share of housing capacity still recovering from a flood + 0.15 x share of crop fields flooded + 0.25 x drought stress on crops (1 - mean water factor) + 0.15 x mud in homes (deposited sediment on housing cells, 40 t = full). The recovering share is max(share flooded this season, 0.6 x last season's recovering share) (`COMMUNITY.floodRecovery`, game-balance): flooded homes take time to dry out and repair, so a flood weighs on safety for about two years, not only in the season the water is up. The townspeople's hazard view uses the same recovering share.
- **Wellbeing** = 0.20 housing + 0.25 food + 0.20 water + 0.20 livelihood + 0.15 safety (1 - hazard). The parts and weights are stored with every state (`wellbeingParts`) so the UI can always show the breakdown.
- **Stakeholders**: farmers (farm income per farm job, irrigation supply ratio, flood and drought exposure), townspeople (wages per resident, household water and how much of it came from tanks and wells, flooded homes and mud) and downstream households outside the map. For them the model computes three indices from the valley's outlet: water access (river flow leaving the valley against the starting landscape's normal for the season), a hazard index (0.6 x outlet sediment against 3 times a normal season + 0.4 x how far the main river's flood peak exceeds bankfull) and a livelihood index from wetland extent and flow. The river beyond the map is not simulated. Each stakeholder has a note assembled from those numbers.

## 10. Explanations, history, replay and objectives

- **Explanations** (`engine/explain.ts`) are assembled only from numbers in the state and parameters. Each carries an `evidence` list with before and after values. Weather is always labelled "(weather)" and described as set by the seed; land condition and decisions are named separately. Harvest changes are compared with the same season a year earlier and decomposed by the log of each factor in the production function (area, soil, water, management, event), so the text can say how much came from rain and how much from soil or choices. Durations and thresholds quoted in the text (maturity times, the tree-product ramp, the departure thresholds) are read from `params.ts`. The downstream note cites the downstream hazard index and the sediment ratio it is computed from. Good outcomes (recoveries, maturing restoration, a village that stayed dry in heavy rain, trees starting to pay) are explained as well as bad ones.
- **History**: one `HistoryPoint` per step plus a pre-game baseline point (`step 0`, labelled year 0 dry). Each point is labelled with the season it simulated and carries the twin's results for the same season (`ref*` fields). `Snapshot` copies layers and fields; terrain is shared.
- **Replay**: the app's saves, loads, branching and Compare rebuild a run with `replayRun` / `replayRunAsync` in `src/state/replay.ts`, which re-run `createInitialState`, `applyAction` and `step` from the action log and skip and report any entry the engine rejects. Branching to Scenario B ("try another future") starts a new game on the same world and seed from the first season. The engine also has its own `replay(world, seed, log, untilStep)` and `branchAt` (`engine/replay.ts`, which throws on an invalid entry) and `serializeState` / `deserializeState` (`serialize.ts`, a lossless full-state round trip); only the determinism tests use those, and they check that replay reproduces the live state exactly.
- **Objectives** (`scenarios/guided.ts`, `evaluateObjectives`): 11 goals.
  1. `housing` Homes for everyone: housing adequacy at least 0.9.
  2. `growth` A growing town: population at least 1.5 times the start.
  3. `food` Food grown here: food self-sufficiency (local food over need, bought food not counted) at least 1.0, averaged over the non-drought seasons of the last 3 years.
  4. `water` Safe water at home: household water security at least 0.9, averaged over the same seasons.
  5. `land` Healthier land: on the hillside farms the map started with and on every cell whose land use differs from the twin's (or whose cover is younger than the twin's, such as a replanted forest), mean soil health at least 0.01 above the twin's on the same cells. A valley left alone scores exactly 0.
  6. `cover` Keep the valley green: forest and wetland cells, agroforestry counting half, at least 95% of the start.
  7. `solvency` A solvent budget: all bills paid in the non-drought seasons of the last 3 years and debt at most half the borrowing limit.
  8. `wellbeing` A good place to live: wellbeing at least 0.9 averaged over the non-drought seasons of the last 3 years, and none of its five parts (homes, food, water, livelihood, safety) averaging below 0.8 over the same seasons.
  9. `drought` Ready for drought: in every season of the most recent drought (a run of consecutive drought seasons), food and household water security both at least 0.6.
  10. `floods` Homes out of the floodwater: no home floods in the whole game, or flooded home-seasons at most 0.9 of the twin's, as a ratio (yours + 1) / (twin + 1).
  11. `erosion` Hold the soil on the hills: erosion index over the last 10 years at most 0.95 of the twin's over the same seasons.

  Goals 3, 4, 7 and 8 skip drought seasons (the drought goal judges those), so a drought late in the game does not fail them whatever the player did; If all 6 seasons were drought the whole window is used. Goals 5, 9 and 11 are compared with the twin or judged on recent seasons so a player can recover from an early mistake, see section 19. Section 21 explains how the targets were set against the strategy bots. The land and cover goals follow the UNCCD idea of land degradation neutrality (no net loss of land condition or cover, with losses balanced by restoration elsewhere; Orr et al. 2017). The land goal compares with the business-as-usual twin in the same weather rather than with the starting map: the weather seed moved the map-wide mean soil health by more (about 0.01 between seeds) than terracing 40 farms did (about 0.0002), so the old comparison with the start was decided by the seed. `endReport` summarises a playthrough.

The planned strategies (all but sprawl, clear-cut, idle and the naive bots) add a waterworks when household demand nears 90% of the capacity they have. The recovery bot plays clear and farm for 6 years, then raises the levy to 2.5 times the starting rate, borrows, builds a reservoir, puts fodder trees on the steep farms and terraces on the sloping ones, replants the cleared forest (steepest first, more each season as money comes in), digs canals to the farms the reservoir can reach, adds canal-fed farms, and once the forest is back plants fodder trees on sloping pasture while the herds outgrow it.

- Clearing upstream forest for farms raises heavy-rain outflow, the flood peak at the village, sediment leaving the valley (more than 20% higher than with agroforestry and terraces) and the number of flooded homes, and loses a larger share of farm value in drought. - Clear-cutting the forest for timber (the bot tries to clear every unprotected forest cell in the first 3 years, then does nothing) earns far less from timber than the clearing costs, so the starting fund fells only about 120 ha before the money runs out. Household water in drought is the same as doing nothing (1.00 in both). With unlimited money (every unprotected tree felled) the costs are much larger, see section 12.

- Intensification grows more food but wears out hillside soil and strains the budget. Sprawl on flat floodplain land earns money but floods more homes and removes forest.
- No strategy is better than another on every axis (money, food, soil, wellbeing, population, downstream sediment, worst-season water, forest, river flow in drought).
- Several different strategies reach a decent end state, they meet different sets of goals, and a bad start (6 years of clearing) can be recovered by switching to restoration: the recovery bot meets more goals than the clear-and-farm bot it started as on both maps (valley 8 against 7, Kaani 7 against 6; tested), with its soil, cover and sediment back near or past where they were.
- These relations also hold on other scenario seeds.

## 12. What it cannot do, and known limitations

- It is not calibrated to any real place. The procedural valley is fictional, and on the real-data Kaani map only the landscape is real (section 20). No result should be used to predict floods, yields, erosion or water supply anywhere.
- **Forests and dry-season flow: what the model actually shows.** In this model trees use more water than grass or bare ground (deeper roots, a higher crop coefficient), and there is no extra recharge under trees. So clearing forest raises the river's flow in a drought. That direction is consistent with paired-catchment experiments, which mostly find that removing forest raises water yield (Bosch and Hewlett 1982). The counter-process is in the model too: freshly cleared bare land is compacted and sheds more storm water and recharges less (section 4, item 5), and the river evaporates less when it is low (item 11). With both in place, a test that fells every unprotected tree on the procedural valley (with unlimited money) finds drought-season flow at the village intake between 1.2 and 3.2 times the do-nothing valley, about 1.9 times on average. These are increases on low flows. The same clearing costs 4.98 million and earns 70 000 in timber, floods about four times as many homes (47 home-seasons against 12), sends more than 13 times the sediment downstream (716 000 t against 52 000 t), lowers soil health (0.585 against 0.727) and lowers wellbeing (0.909 against 0.924 averaged over the 20 years). Household water in drought does not change (1.00 in both), because roof tanks, wells and the river already meet the need on this valley. Some studies find the opposite effect on dry-season flow where soils are badly degraded after clearing (Bruijnzeel 2004); the model represents that only through the temporary compaction of bare land, so it cannot show a case where clearing dries up the river. Read the dry-season flow result as one side of a contested relationship, not a general rule.
- Which half of the drought goal binds depends on the map. On the procedural valley, which starts with a waterworks, household water is rarely short in drought at the starting town size, so the goal is usually decided by food. On Kaani, which starts with no waterworks, household water is the first thing to fail (30% of need at the start, 12% in the worst drought season if the player builds nothing), so the goal is decided by water first and by food once a waterworks and a reservoir are in place.
- Seasonal steps hide storm-scale processes. Runoff uses a fixed storm-size distribution; floods use a screening rule on HAND, not hydraulics. Peak flows and flood extents are indicative only.
- One hydrologic soil group (B) everywhere; no soil texture, salinity, nutrients, pests, groundwater flow between cells, snow, or climate trend.
- Erosion is an index with the RUSLE structure and game-scaled magnitudes. It is not validated soil loss.
- Soil health is a single index standing in for organic matter, structure and fertility together.
- The economy is a single community fund with fixed prices (apart from the falling timber price within a season). There are no markets outside the valley, no inflation and no household-level distribution.
- Population responds to simple attractiveness rules; the migration wave is a seeded curve, not a demographic projection.
- Wellbeing weights are a value judgement made for the game and shown openly to players.
- The guided weather guarantees a drought and a heavy-rain season by design.
- The river beyond the map is not simulated; downstream households are represented only by indices computed at the outlet.

## 13. Which parameters are approximations

Evidence-informed (structure or magnitude from the named source, still simplified): the TR-55 curve numbers and Ia = 0.2 S, the SWAT slope and moisture adjustments, FAO-56 Kc magnitudes and the depletion fraction p = 0.5, open-water evaporation, the FAO-33 yield response (Ky 1.25), the RUSLE K magnitude and its direction with soil condition, the Moore and Burch LS form, the exponential C form, the AH-537 contouring P table by slope (0.5 to 0.9), the MUSLE runoff exponent 0.56 in the run-on factor, silt density.

Game-balance (chosen for play, not measured): everything else, including all costs, prices, incomes, the timber market, jobs, population rules, wellbeing weights, objectives, rooting factors, compaction effects, recharge and baseflow rates, run-on shares, the channel wetted-share reference, wetland and reservoir rules, the shape constants of the reservoir trap curve, the P limit above 25% slope, sealed shares, flood thresholds, the erosion soft ceiling and the soil-loss taper, soil health rates and maxima, grazing and forest product values, vegetation rates, the understory rule and maturity times, potential yields, food need per person, roof tank and well rules, irrigation reach, lift and diversion capacity, the calibration targets and the weather multipliers. The tag on each parameter in `params.ts` is the authoritative list.

## 14. The business-as-usual twin and run-on erosion (`engine/season.ts`)

Alongside the player's valley, the engine runs a **twin**: the starting land use with no decisions at all, through the same weather. Its soil, vegetation and water stores still evolve (it runs the same hydrology, soil and vegetation models), its population stays at the starting population, and its waterworks always run at full service. It never reads player state: tests play four different strategies and check that the twin's layers and results are identical in all of them. When the player has made no decisions, the twin and the player's valley give identical results.

The twin is used for three things:

1. **Run-on erosion factor.** For each cell, R is multiplied by
   ((own twin runoff + surface water arriving from upslope + 5) / (own twin runoff + twin's surface water arriving from upslope + 5))^0.56, bounded to 0.5..2.
   The cell's own runoff is taken from the twin on both sides, because the cell's own cover is already in C; only extra (or reduced) water arriving from upslope changes the factor. The 0.56 exponent on runoff is the one in MUSLE (Williams 1975); the 5 mm offset and the bounds are game-balance. It is exactly 1 everywhere when nothing upstream has changed, above 1 below cleared or sealed land, and below 1 below wetlands and restored slopes that hold water back. This is how clearing a hillside makes the fields below it erode faster.
2. **The floods and erosion goals** (section 19): flooded home-seasons and total erosion are compared with the twin's in the same weather.
3. **History**: every `HistoryPoint` carries the twin's erosion, sediment, upstream runoff, flood ratio, flooded homes and soil health (`ref*` fields), so charts can show "what the starting village would have had in the same weather".

In the frozen pre-game year the twin is provably the player's valley (same layers, stores, population and service level), so the engine computes that season once and shares it.

## 15. Spin-up and per-map calibration (`engine/index.ts`)

`createInitialState` prepares each map in four stages:

1. **Land spin-up** (6 model years). Each year runs hydrology, the erosion index and the vegetation model for a normal wet and dry season. Then each cell's soil health is solved for the level where a year's losses equal a year's recovery: erosion loss (scaled to the long-run erosivity of the Markov weather, not just normal seasons) plus farming and grazing pressure against recovery toward the cover's maximum. The balance rec x (SHmax - SH) = (alpha x K(SH) + pressure) x taper(SH) has one crossing between the floor and the maximum (the left side falls and the right side rises with SH), found by bisection; it is repeated each year as vegetation adjusts. Because the taper is 0 at the floor, no cell with any recovery settles on it: on Kaani the lowest starting soil health on open land is 0.11 (before this solve, 41 Kaani cells started on the 0.05 floor). A little seeded texture (plus or minus 0.015) is added at the end. The result: starting soil is in balance with its land use, so the valley neither improves nor declines on its own.
2. **Water spin-up** (3 normal years) settles soil water, groundwater and wetland stores. From it come the bankfull capacity (section 2), the downstream reference flow and sediment for each season, the reference erosion, and the normal groundwater level per cell that wells are judged against (section 16).
3. **Calibration** (`params.CALIBRATION`, game-balance), so every map starts with the same pressures:
   - **Household scale.** A normal-weather year of the starting farmland is simulated (frozen: indicators only), and so are three designed drought seasons (wet, dry, wet, at mid-range draws). Homes then hold more people, between 1 and 2.5 times the base rule, until either the normal year grows 1.25 times what the residents need, or the worst two consecutive drought seasons grow only 50% of need, whichever comes first. (The drought target was 42% until bought food was made a smaller and paid crutch; at 42% no strategy could meet the drought goal on Kaani any more.) The drought bound was added because a map whose farms are generous in normal years but fragile in drought would otherwise start with a drought goal no strategy can meet. On the procedural valley the unscaled normal balance is 1.31 and the unscaled drought balance 0.41, so the drought bound holds the scale at its floor of 1 (25, 55 and 90 residents per home). On Kaani see section 20.
   - **Levy.** With the scaled population, a second frozen year sets the cooperative levy (between 5% and 30%) so the starting village saves 3% of the starting fund (9 000) in a normal year. On the procedural valley it comes out near 11.5% (0.115).
   - Both are deterministic (no random draws) and stored in `state.calibration` with the balances they produced. `householdScaleBy` records which rule set the scale. Tests check determinism, the bounds and the targets on three procedural maps and on Kaani.
4. **Pre-game year** (frozen) fills fields, totals and community indicators so the first screen shows real values; the budget is reset to the starting fund.

**Cost.** On a laptop in Node, `createInitialState` takes about 290 to 370 ms (it includes the three-season drought probe); one step takes about 16 to 20 ms (median) on both maps. The performance test (`__tests__/invariants.test.ts`) takes the best median of up to three rounds of 12 steps. By default it fails only above 500 ms, which catches order-of-magnitude regressions without failing on a busy machine; with `AMORIA_PERF=1` on a quiet machine it enforces the project's 50 ms budget (the spec asks for a bounded step cost and gives no number; 50 ms is ours). Measured median: 18 ms per step at a load average of 1.5. Because the UI rebuilds saved games by replaying the action log, a 20-year replay costs about one initial state plus 40 steps, under a second.

## 16. Household water: piped supply, roof tanks and shallow wells (`models/hydrology.ts`)

Household demand = population x 18.25 m3 a season (about 100 litres a person a day for homes, livestock and small business). It is met in this order:

1. **Roof tanks** on every home cell catch rain on about 12 m2 of roof per resident with a runoff share of 0.8, up to 3.5 m3 per resident a season and never more than that home's demand. The water caught never reaches the ground (it is a withdrawal in the ledger).
2. **Piped water.** Each waterworks takes water from its adjacent river cell (the one with the largest catchment), treats up to 36 000 m3 a season (enough for about 2 000 people; scaled by the budget service level), and never takes more than 30% of the flow there (an environmental-flow rule). When the river falls short, a reservoir covers the gap, but only through spare treatment capacity. With no waterworks there is no piped water.
3. **Shallow wells** near each home cover what tanks and pipes did not, drawing on the shallow groundwater of the home cell and its 8 neighbours. Their reliability = stored groundwater there / its normal level from spin-up (capped at 1), so wells fail as the water table falls in a long drought. A well can meet at most 30% x reliability of the home's demand and never takes more than half of the stored water in a season. Water drawn is removed from those cells' groundwater.

Water security uses all three. Tests check that with no waterworks household water is exactly tanks plus wells and the ledger still closes, that wells yield less as local groundwater falls below its normal level (and less when the same groundwater is judged against a higher normal level), that wells never exceed their share, and that with a working waterworks in a normal season wells are not needed.

## 17. Grazing and forest livelihoods (`models/agriculture.ts`)

- **Grazing.** Each open grassland cell yields livestock value of 22 a ha a season x min(1, vegetation / 0.68) x soil factor (x 0.35 when flooded). It goes to herding households, not through the levy. Grazing also costs soil: 0.0005 soil health a season before recovery (section 5). Converting pasture to fields or homes removes its grazing value; tests check that the loss equals the converted cells' value.
- **Grazing pressure** (`models/grazing.ts`, `AGRI.livestock`, game-balance). Herds grow with the town, more slowly than the population because many new residents take non-farm work: herd / reference herd = (population / reference population)^0.5. The reference is the starting homes full (housing capacity x 1.05), so the starting pasture is taken to be in balance with that village and a valley left alone never overgrazes. Fodder land counts each open pasture cell as 1, a fodder-tree agroforestry plot as 1.5 (tree fodder plus the grass under it), cropland as 0.3 (crop residues grazed after harvest), forest as 0.15 and wetland as 0.4. Pressure = (herd / reference herd) / (fodder land / starting fodder land), between 1/3 and 3; overgrazing = max(0, pressure - 1). Overgrazing multiplies grazing soil loss by 1 + 4 x overgrazing and divides the grass cover target by 1 + 0.6 x overgrazing, so overgrazed pasture holds less cover, erodes more (C rises), sends more sediment to the waterworks intake (higher treatment cost, section 8) and earns herders less. The twin uses its own fixed population and land, so its pressure stays at or below 1. Overgrazing is one of the main drivers of dryland degradation named by the UNCCD; the numbers here are not measured stocking rates. On Kaani the homes-only strategy ends at a pressure of 1.26 and its pasture soils fall enough to fail the land goal; tests check that both naive growth bots end above 1.15 on both maps and fail the land and erosion goals, while idle stays at or below 1.
- **Forest products.** Each standing forest cell gives households 18 a ha a season x min(1, vegetation / 0.88): fuelwood, fodder, wild foods, honey, building poles. It also goes to households, not through the levy, and stops when the forest is cleared. Without it, clearing forest for pasture added herding income and nothing was lost, so clear-cutting raised livelihoods.

The grazing and forest values are game-balance. They enter household income and so livelihood (section 9) and the food import cap.

## 18. Irrigation rules (`models/landUse.ts`, `models/hydrology.ts`)

- **Where.** A field can be irrigated when a source can feed a canal to it: a waterworks (through its river intake) or a reservoir cell within 8 cells (800 m, Chebyshev distance), with the field no more than 12 m above the source's water level (a small pump lift; elevations on the filled surface). Otherwise the action is refused with the reason: no source, too far, or too high above the source. Fields can still be farmed rainfed there.
- **How much.** Irrigation demand on each irrigated, cropped field = max(0, ETm - (soil water + 0.85 x season rain)) / 0.8 efficiency, estimated before the season's routing.
- **Capacity.** Each waterworks headworks can divert up to 80 000 m3 a season of raw water for canals (scaled by service level), separate from its treated household capacity and never treated, so irrigation pays canal operation but no treatment cost. River diversions share the 30% environmental-flow limit with household abstraction, and households come first.
- **Reservoir.** Irrigation can draw on a reservoir only above a domestic reserve of 30% of its capacity, which is kept for households.
- **Delivery.** Delivered water reaches the root zone at 80% efficiency (the rest seeps to groundwater); the crop uses it up to its unmet ETm, and any surplus stays in the soil.

## 19. Resilience goals and recovering from mistakes (`scenarios/guided.ts`, `resilienceMeasures`, `landGain`)

The definition of done asks that a player can recover from bad decisions, so no goal is lost for good after the first years:

- **Ready for drought** (`drought`): the lowest of min(food security, household water security) over the seasons of the most recent drought episode (consecutive drought seasons); met when at least 0.6. It is 1 until the first drought arrives. A player who was caught out by the first drought and then built a waterworks, a reservoir and canals gets the goal back at the next one. `worstDroughtSecurity` (the worst drought season of the whole game) is still reported for the end report and the bots.
- **Homes out of the floodwater** (`floods`): (flooded home-seasons + 1) / (the twin's flooded home-seasons + 1), summed over the whole game, or 0 when no home has flooded at all; met when at most 0.9. The +1 keeps the ratio defined when the twin has none. This one stays whole-game on purpose: heavy-rain seasons are rare (two to four in 20 years), so a 10-year window often held none and let a valley left alone pass on some seeds. Flooding is still recoverable in practice, because the count is against the twin and moving homes off the floodplain stops new flood seasons.
- **Hold the soil on the hills** (`erosion`): erosion index over the last 10 years (`OBJECTIVES.resilienceWindowSeasons` = 20 seasons) / the twin's over the same seasons; met when at most 0.95. Early clearing is forgiven once it has been restored and the trees have grown.
- **Healthier land** (`land`, `landGain`): see section 10. Because it is measured against the twin, weather cannot decide it: a valley left alone scores 0 on every seed (to within 1e-4), the agroforestry and funded balanced bots score above 0.03 on every one of 20 seeds on both maps, and clearing for farms scores below 0.

Tests recompute each value from the history and check that the goal reports exactly that value, its target and whether it is met; a separate test makes the first drought a disaster and the last one fine and checks that the drought goal is met, and that an unpaid bill in a drought season does not fail solvency. The UI shows the floods and erosion goals as ratios to the twin.

## 20. Running the model on the real-data Kaani map

The default world players start on is `kaani-machakos` (`src/data/registry.ts`, the first real world): a 6.4 km square of hill country east of Machakos town, Kenya, at the same 64 x 64 grid of 100 m cells as the valley. Its elevation (Copernicus DEM), land cover (ESA WorldCover 2021), roads (OpenStreetMap) and derived stream lines come from real data; the world file's `provenance` field has the details. Everything else is the generic model in this document.

**What is not Kaani's own.**
- **Climate.** The map runs the same generic two-season semi-arid climate as the valley (section 3): one wet season of about 420 mm and one dry season of about 110 mm, about 530 mm a year, with the same seeded drought and heavy-rain sequence. This is not the measured Machakos climate. Machakos has two rainy seasons a year (the long rains around March to May and the short rains around October to December) with dry spells between them; the model has one wet and one dry season, and no station data were used.
- **Soils, rates and prices.** One hydrologic soil group everywhere, and every coefficient in `params.ts` is the same on both maps. No Kaani yields, incomes, water use or erosion were measured or fitted.
- **Settlement.** Homes and the starting population come from the built-up cells in the land cover, through the same housing rule. The real village has no mapped waterworks, so none is placed.

**Calibration it produces** (section 15, seed 42): unscaled normal-year food balance 1.80 and unscaled drought balance 0.57. The drought bound sets the household scale to 1.14 (28, 63 and 102 residents per home at density 1, 2 and 3), so the starting population is 2 023; the normal-year food balance after scaling is 1.59 and the worst two drought seasons grow 50% of need. Without the drought bound the scale would be 1.44 and no bundled strategy could meet the drought goal. The levy comes out at 6.3% (0.063), the lower part of its range, because the farmland is large relative to the village's running costs; the normal-year surplus is the same 9 000 as on the valley.

**Starting conditions.** Mean soil health on open land is 0.61 (the valley: 0.73), lowest 0.11, with no cell on the 0.05 floor. About 13% of the land is forest (the valley: 46%). With no waterworks, households start on roof tanks and shallow wells only: they get 31% of the water they need at the start (a normal dry season) and between 12% and 49% each season for the rest of the game if nothing is built. Building a waterworks is the first thing most strategies do.

**Erosion.** The steep grassland and cropland on Kaani erode much more than the valley's: in a normal wet season 15 open-land cells pass the 150 knee of the soft ceiling (none on the valley) and in the heavy-rain season 230 (14 on the valley), but none is within the saturated part of the curve (where its response has halved) in either season. Clearing the 60 steepest forest cells raises their normal-season erosion from about 400 to about 9 200 game tonnes, and heavy rain gives 2.97 times the normal-season erosion on those cleared cells (3.32 when they are left as forest). Under the old hard cap the cleared ratio was 1.13. Tests on Kaani check that under 1% of erodible land is saturated in a normal season, that heavy rain erodes every cell with C > 0 more than a normal season, that no cell starts on the soil floor, and that the cleared ratio stays above 1.5.

What this shows on Kaani:
- Every one of the 11 goals is met by at least one strategy, no strategy meets all 11, and no strategy is at least as good as every other on every goal (funded balanced meets 9). The drought goal is met only by the drought-ready strategy (0.60): with a waterworks alone, household water still falls to about a quarter of need in the driest drought season, because the river is small and only 30% of its flow may be taken; a reservoir carries wet-season water into it. Food then decides the rest: the balanced strategy also has a reservoir and secure water but a larger town to feed, and its worst drought food security is 0.56, just short.
- **Growth alone no longer wins.** Building homes and nothing for the land (homes only) grows the town most after sprawl (3 759 people) but its farms grow only 95% of the need by the end, it spends about twice as much on bought food as the drought-ready strategy, its herds overgraze (pressure 1.26), its soil falls and its erosion is 8% above the twin, so it fails the food, land, drought and erosion goals and its households have among the lowest livelihoods (0.795 on average against 0.856 for drought-ready; the two bots that raised the levy, recovery and funded balanced, are lower still at 0.799 and 0.779, which is the price of their richer fund). Adding plain farms (homes and farms) fixes food but not the land, the erosion or the drought, and ties the drought-ready strategy at 8 goals; funded balanced meets 9. Section 21 has the full goal table.
- The same tradeoffs as on the valley hold, with smaller effects because there is much less forest to clear: clearing upstream for farms raises hillside quick runoff in heavy rain, the flood peak at the village (against the land left alone) and sediment, and loses a larger share of farm value in drought than agroforestry, while growing more food. Restoration spends most of the fund early and ends with better soil, less sediment and much safer household water than doing nothing. Clear-cutting removes forest, raises sediment and erosion, and earns less than it costs. Sprawl on the flats is the only strategy that floods homes. A bad start can be recovered: the replanted cells erode less than half as much as the same cells kept as farms. The map-wide erosion of the recovery bot stays above the twin's (1.08), because it raised the levy, its town grew to 3 418 people and the bigger herds wear the pasture (grazing pressure 1.22); growth has a land cost even for a town that restores.
- **Intensification is a debt trap here, by design.** High inputs cost the fund 55 a ha a season, but with a 6.3% levy the fund gets back only a small share of the extra harvest, and on these dry hillsides the extra harvest is small (22 296 t against 21 513 t for doing nothing, 4% more). The fund runs dry, unpaid bills are added to the debt and charge interest every season, and it ends with a debt of about 565 000, more than the 400 000 borrowing limit (unpaid bills are owed, not new loans). Budget shortages also cut the input program and waterworks operation; inputs are billed only for the share actually delivered, so a shortfall season costs less and also yields less. The lesson is that a high-input program paid from a common fund that captures little of the return does not pay on dry land. A player sees the farm-input bill ("seed and fertiliser") in the budget explanations; the bot never stops, a player could.
- Floods matter less on Kaani than on the valley: in this model's flood screening the starting homes sit above the floodwater, so only homes built on low flats flood.

## 21. Goal targets and the strategy bots

The goal targets (`params.OBJECTIVES`) are game-balance. They were set against the spread of the bots (sections 11 and 20) so that every goal is a decision. Every new game in the app starts from a random seed, so the goals are checked over 20 fixed scenario seeds on both maps, not just seed 42 (`__tests__/seeds.valley.test.ts` and `seeds.kaani.test.ts`, shared code in `seedsShared.ts`):

- the best land-aware bot (funded balanced) meets at least 7 goals on at least 90% of seeds; it meets 7 to 10 on all 20 on both maps;
- doing nothing fails at least 5 goals on every seed (Kaani 7 or 8, the valley 5 to 8);
- no bot meets all 11 on any seed;
- the land goal sits at 0 for a valley left alone, above 0.03 for restoration and below 0 for clearing on every seed;
- the scenario intro reports the number of drought seasons the seed brings, and the seeds really differ (7 to 17 drought seasons in 40).

Why these values:
- **Food grown here, at least 1.0.** Bought food is capped and paid for (section 9), so food security alone stayed near 1 for every strategy and never asked anyone to farm. Self-sufficiency of 1.0 asks the valley to feed itself in normal years; homes-only and sprawl fail it on both maps, and the agroforestry bot fails it on the valley (its staple yields are half), a real tradeoff against its income and soil.
- **Wellbeing at least 0.9 with no part below 0.8.** At the old target of 0.65 every bot passed. The weakest-part rule fails a valley where one part is left far behind: livelihood under homes-only growth or under a high levy, or water without a waterworks.
- **Land at least 0.01 above the twin.** The value is a mean over the cells the player changed plus the hillside farms, so a real program of restoration moves it by 0.05 to 0.16, and clearing by -0.03 to -0.26. Terraces alone move it a little (the drought-ready bot's 40 terraces: about 0.02 on Kaani and 0.05 on the valley).
- **Floods: none, or at most 0.9 of the twin.** With "no more than the twin" a valley left alone met the goal by definition. Now it has to make homes safer than the starting village would have been (on Kaani, where the starting homes do not flood, the goal is not to start flooding them).
- **Drought at 0.6** on the last drought. The margin on Kaani is small: the drought-ready bot reaches 0.60.

**Known exception: household water on the procedural valley.** The valley starts with a waterworks, and roof tanks and wells cover the rest, so every bot ends with household water at 1.0 there. On that map household water is tested only by the drought goal. The test records this exception explicitly, so it will fail if a change makes the valley's water goal bite.
